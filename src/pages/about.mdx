**Dr. Eric Janusson** — analytical chemist, mass spectrometrist, and scientific software developer.

I have spent more than a decade in mass spectrometry, across academic research, regulated industry
laboratories, and open source. My work has followed a single question — what does it take for a
chemical measurement to be trustworthy? — from the physics of the electrospray droplet, to watching
reactions happen in real time, to the automated pipelines and software that modern analytical
science depends on.

## Research

My doctoral work at the University of Victoria (2012–2017) began at the source itself: the
electrospray droplet. I studied how solvent composition controls the surface activity of aggregate
ions, and led the work showing that spray geometry shapes ion yield — the physical factors that
decide which ions reach the detector, independent of analyte concentration. From there my research
turned to chemistry in motion: using pressurized sample infusion ESI-MS, I followed the activation
of a palladium precatalyst by phosphine ligands in real time. My dissertation developed the mass
spectrometry techniques for monitoring reactions as they happen — a foundation that later supported
step-by-step observation of a catalytic amination.

At the University of Glasgow (2017–2020), as a postdoctoral research associate, I moved from
individual measurements to high-dimensional ones. Coupling travelling-wave ion mobility with
high-resolution mass spectrometry, I studied polyoxometalate self-assembly — clusters growing from
chains into rings into spheres — and isomer-selective synthesis driven by carbohydrate reducing
agents. The data quickly outgrew what instrument software could interpret, so I wrote automated
Python pipelines to process it reproducibly. That work convinced me that modern analytical
chemistry is as much a computational discipline as a physical one.

## Industry

Industry asked for a different kind of rigor: methods that survive audits, not only peer review. At
Delic Labs (2021–2022), as Lead Chemist, I took a new analytical laboratory from empty benches to a
federally authorized and licensed analytical program — commissioning instruments, developing and
validating methods, and building an ISO/IEC 17025-aligned QA/QC framework through the pandemic
years — then built the untargeted DIA-LC-MS/MS profiling pipeline that annotated more than 9,500
spectra across botanical products and regulated forensic samples.

At TMC Manufacturing (2024–2026), as Principal Scientist for Analytical Development & Laboratory
Operations, I brought trace-element analysis in-house for ultra-high-purity stable-isotope
production — authoring and justifying a $515k ICP-MS capital procurement, developing and validating
a sub-2 ppm potentiometric assay supporting ≥99.999% (5N+) purity certification, and building the
trace-element methods behind it. In both roles the lesson was the same: reliability is a property
of the whole system — instrument, method, documentation, and data handling — not of any single
measurement.

## Software

Today my focus is the software layer of analytical science. [MassFlow](https://github.com/janusson/MassFlow)
is a config-first, vendor-agnostic Python toolkit for MS/MS annotation: spectral similarity scoring,
reproducible spectral libraries, and interactive molecular networking.
[MSMCP](https://github.com/janusson/MSMCP) gives AI agents direct, queryable access to raw and
processed mass spectrometry data through the Model Context Protocol. Both are open source, MIT
licensed, and documented — built so the methods I spent a decade developing do not depend on a
single vendor's software to remain useful.
