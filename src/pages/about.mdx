# About Me

<a href="/cv/" class="btn btn--cv btn--large">Download CV (PDF) &rarr;</a>

Mass spectrometry answers questions by counting ions. Every structure assignment, every mechanism, every certified concentration rests on the assumption that the measurement behaves — that the signal means what the software says it means. That assumption is not free. For fourteen years, the work behind this site has been one evolving question: what does it take for a mass spectrometer's answer to be trustworthy enough to act on?

The question has changed form four times, each phase answering the one before it: *measurement* — how the instrument behaves; *information* — what the data means; *systems* — how laboratories operate on that meaning; *infrastructure* — how the whole thing becomes reusable code. The sections below follow that arc; the underlying record is on the [Publications](/publications/) page.

## How does the measurement behave?

At the University of Victoria (2012–2017), the question was the electrospray itself — the process that decides which ions reach the detector. Electrospray response depends on factors unrelated to analyte concentration: which ions occupy the droplet surface, and how the spray is arranged in space. Two studies established that. Solvent composition controls the surface activity of aggregate ions and therefore their response ([*Int. J. Mass Spectrom.*, 2014](/publications/#fundamentals)); spray geometry measurably shapes ion yield ([*Int. J. Mass Spectrom.*, 2015](/publications/#fundamentals)).

The result was methodological. Before a measurement can be interpreted, it must be understood — response is a physical phenomenon with knobs that can be turned, measured, and accounted for.

## What can we learn from the measurement?

With the measurement under control, it could be pointed at chemistry in motion. Pressurized sample infusion ESI-MS followed the activation of Pd<sub>2</sub>(dba)<sub>3</sub> by phosphine ligands — the moment a precatalyst becomes the active species — in real time ([*Chem. Commun.*, 2016](/publications/#reaction-monitoring)). The doctoral dissertation generalized the approach into techniques for watching reactions as they happen, and the same discipline was turned to selectivity — charge-tagged disulfides made thiols selectively visible ([*Analyst*, 2016](/publications/#applied)) — and to real matrices, with phenol-selective analysis of jet fuel ([*Analyst*, 2017](/publications/#applied)).

Real-time information changed what a mechanism argument could be: not inference from products, but observation of the species themselves.

## How do we handle complex measurements at scale?

At the University of Glasgow (2017–2020), single measurements stopped being the problem. An automated synthetic-analytical platform — inline microfluidic synthesis coupled directly to electrospray, travelling-wave ion mobility, and time-of-flight mass spectrometry — produced high-dimensional data: retention time, drift time, m/z, and intensity for every species at every timepoint, as polyoxometalate clusters assembled from chains into rings into spheres in real time.

The instrument software could extract peaks. It could not track a chain-to-ring transition across a time series, or carry an assignment from one timepoint to the next. The answer was code — the first automated Python analytical pipelines, built to process TWIMS-HRMS data reproducibly. They resolved self-assembly mechanisms ([*Chem. Commun.*, 2019](/publications/#inorganic-pom)) and followed a catalytic amination step by step ([*Chem. Commun.*, 2019](/publications/#reaction-monitoring)).

Data at this scale cannot be read; it must be processed. Processing that lives in one-off scripts is not reproducible science; it is archaeology.

## How do we make analytical systems reliable in the real world?

Two industrial engagements tested the answer under regulation. At Delic Labs (2021–2022), the work was commissioning a federally authorized analytical facility from empty benches — instruments, ISO/IEC 17025-aligned SOPs, and a QA/QC framework — with an untargeted DIA-LC-MS/MS profiling pipeline that annotated more than 9,500 spectra in botanical products. At TMC Manufacturing (2024–2026), elemental analysis was internalized for ultra-high-purity stable-isotope production: a $515k ICP-MS capital procurement authored and justified, a sub-2 ppm potentiometric assay developed and validated, and trace-element methods supporting ≥99.999% purity certification.

Both engagements made explicit what the earlier phases had only implied: reliability is a systems property. A method survives an audit only if everything around it — documentation, controls, data handling — survives too. The pipelines from Glasgow, disciplined by regulation, became laboratory infrastructure.

## Can we turn analytical knowledge into reusable computational infrastructure?

Since 2026, the answer has been public. [MassFlow](https://github.com/janusson/MassFlow) is a config-first, vendor-agnostic Python toolkit for MS/MS annotation — custom spectral similarity scoring, version-controlled spectral libraries, and interactive molecular networking. [MSMCP](https://github.com/janusson/MSMCP) is a Model Context Protocol server that exposes raw and processed mass spectrometry datasets as queryable tools for AI agents. Both are open source and documented, built so the annotation layer outlives any instrument vendor's software.

The arc is deliberate. The droplet physics of the first section, the real-time kinetics of the second, the high-dimensional pipelines of the third, and the regulated discipline of the fourth are now installed artifacts — reproducible, versioned, reviewable. The question that began as *how does the measurement behave?* is now *how does the field run on it?* That question, and its answer, keep evolving.
