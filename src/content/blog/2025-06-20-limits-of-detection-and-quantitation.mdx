If a method has to report an impurity at a specified concentration, I start there. Can the method
detect it in the sample matrix? Can it also measure it with acceptable bias and precision? Those
are different questions.

## Limit of detection (LOD)

The LOD estimates the lowest amount or concentration that the method can detect under defined
conditions. [ICH Q2(R2)](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/q2r2-validation-analytical-procedures)
describes several ways to estimate the lower limits of an analytical procedure. One uses the
standard deviation of the response, $\sigma$, and the slope of a calibration curve, $m$.

$$
\mathrm{LOD} \approx \frac{3.3\sigma}{m}
$$

You can estimate $\sigma$ from replicate blanks or from calibration data near the lower end of the
range. With a linear calibration, the residual standard deviation is one option. The estimate is
only as relevant as the samples and calibration used to calculate it. Where it is appropriate to
measure signal-to-noise directly, a ratio around 3:1 is another starting point.

The slope is the calibration sensitivity. The estimated LOD depends on both that slope and the
variability of the response. A steep curve can still give a poor detection limit if the blank or
low-level samples are noisy.

## Limit of quantitation (LOQ)

The LOQ is the lowest amount or concentration that the method can quantify with acceptable accuracy
and precision. Using the same slope and standard deviation, the corresponding estimate is

$$
\mathrm{LOQ} \approx \frac{10\sigma}{m}.
$$

For a signal-to-noise estimate, the usual starting point is 10:1. To establish a working LOQ, measure
replicate samples at or near the proposed limit and compare recovery and precision with predefined
acceptance criteria. Use the intended sample matrix or a justified surrogate and put the replicates
through the full preparation.

## Checking the estimate in the lab

- Prepare blanks and low-level samples using the same procedure as routine samples. Carryover,
  reagent contamination, and matrix effects can change what you can see near the limit.
- Choose the calculation that matches how the signal is measured. A chromatographic baseline gives
  different information from replicate preparations.
- Record the matrix, how you estimated the limit, and what the low-level replicates actually showed.
  If the method needs to quantify at a specification, confirm the performance there.
